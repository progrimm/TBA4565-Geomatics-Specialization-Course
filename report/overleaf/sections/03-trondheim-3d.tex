\section{Trondheim 3D}\label{sec:p2}

\subsection{Purpose}

CesiumJS \citep{cesiumjs} is one of the most used libraries for 3D maps and digital globes on the web, and I wanted to learn it. I chose to build a 3D model of Trondheim, a city I know, with terrain, buildings and one open Norwegian dataset. The dataset is DSB's register of public shelters (\emph{tilfluktsrom}), because it leads to a simple practical question: which shelter is closest, and how far is the walk?

The app shows Trondheim with Cesium World Terrain and Cesium OSM Buildings, and the shelters as points (\cref{fig:p2-overview}). A control panel has three preset camera views (Midtbyen, Nidarosdomen and Bakklandet) and switches for the layers.

\subsection{The 3D scene}

A CesiumJS app is built around a \code{Viewer}, which contains the globe, the camera and the default imagery. Terrain and buildings are streamed from Cesium ion and need an access token.

The buildings are a 3D Tiles dataset \citep{3dtiles}. The city is divided into tiles at several levels of detail, and Cesium only loads the detailed tiles where the camera needs them. The setting \code{maximumScreenSpaceError} controls how much detail is loaded. I tested the value 32 against the default of 16 over Midtbyen. With 32, the building tiles used 33.5\,\% less memory, but many small buildings in the centre were missing, so I kept the default.

Cesium has two ways of drawing data. The shelters are \emph{entities} loaded from GeoJSON, which come with selection and an info box with the shelter's number, capacity and address. The buildings are a \emph{primitive}, a lower-level type meant for large datasets. When the user clicks the map, the app first checks whether an object was hit. A click on a building or a shelter selects it, while a click on the ground gives a position on the terrain, which starts the shelter search below.

\subsection{Coordinates and heights}

DSB publishes the shelters through an OGC Web Feature Service (WFS) \citep{ogcwfs} as GML in EUREF89 / UTM zone 32N (EPSG:25832). A script fetches them once, converts them to longitude and latitude with proj4js, and saves them as GeoJSON in the app. The 12 shelters have room for 5\,268 people in total.

Heights were harder. Cesium measures heights above the WGS~84 ellipsoid, while Norwegian heights (NN2000) are measured from a surface close to the geoid, which lies about 40\,m above the ellipsoid in Trondheim. If an NN2000 height is used directly in Cesium, the point ends up about 40\,m too low. Each preset view therefore aims at a landmark whose ground height I looked up in Kartverket's height service, plus 40\,m. The camera is placed relative to this target in the target's local east--north--up frame, given as a direction and a distance, so the landmark is always in the middle of the view.

\subsection{Nearest-shelter analysis}

The walking network was downloaded from OpenStreetMap with the Overpass API, using only roads and paths that are open to pedestrians. In the app, the network becomes a graph: the OSM nodes are vertices, and each road segment is an edge weighted by its length on the ellipsoid. When the user clicks the ground, the click and the shelters are snapped to their nearest node, and Dijkstra's algorithm \citep{dijkstra1959} searches outwards from the click until it reaches the first shelter. The route is drawn on the ground, and the panel shows both the walking distance and the straight-line distance. I chose to run the search in the browser instead of using a routing service, because the service's API key would be visible to anyone in the app's code, and its free daily quota could run out.

\Cref{fig:p2-route} shows why the walking distance matters. From a point south of Nidarosdomen, the nearest shelter is 490\,m away in a straight line, but the river is in the way, and the walk over Gamle Bybro is 684\,m.

To see how often this happens, I ran the search from 900 points in a regular grid over the area of the shelters. A shelter could be reached from 884 of them. For 168 of these points (19\,\%), the nearest shelter on foot was a different one than the nearest in a straight line. The median walking distance was 1.34 times the straight-line distance.

\subsection{Limitations}

The analysis does not consider hills, stairs, barriers or how many people each shelter can take. The click is snapped to the nearest node, not the nearest point on a road, and the short connection to that node is a straight line. The 40\,m geoid offset is an approximation for Trondheim, not a value from a geoid model. The search needs a click on the map, so it cannot be used with a keyboard.
