\section{Projection Lab}\label{sec:p1}

\subsection{Purpose}

Many of the world maps people see use the Mercator projection, where Greenland looks about as large as Africa. In September 2026, the UN General Assembly adopted the resolution ``Correct the Map'', which recommends equal-area world maps, such as the Equal Earth projection, instead of Mercator. The resolution is not binding \citep{un-correctmap}. At the same time, France announced that it would drop Mercator in favour of the equal-area Eckert IV projection \citep{vg-verdenskart}. This is why I chose this project: I wanted to see for myself how large the difference between Mercator and an equal-area map really is, and to show it to people without GIS background. The app shows one world map with a slider that morphs from Mercator to Eckert IV (\cref{fig:p1-endpoints}). Clicking a country shows how much Mercator exaggerates its area, and a chart shows how the exaggeration depends on latitude.

\subsection{Theory}

The Mercator projection is conformal: it keeps angles and local shapes. To do this, it stretches the map by the same factor in the east--west and north--south directions, and this factor grows with latitude $\varphi$ as $\sec\varphi$ \citep{snyder1987}. Areas are therefore scaled by
\begin{equation}
  k_{\text{area}} = \sec^2\varphi ,
\end{equation}
which is 1 at the equator, 4 at 60\dg{} and 33.2 at 80\dg{}. At the poles the scale is infinite, so Mercator maps are cut off, usually at about 85.05\dg{}. Eckert IV is an equal-area projection: every area keeps its true relative size, but shapes are sheared towards the edges of the map. No flat map can keep both shapes and areas.

\subsection{Method}

\paragraph{The map.} In D3, a projection is a function from longitude and latitude to screen coordinates. The app blends the two projections by interpolating their outputs, $(1-t)\cdot\text{Mercator} + t\cdot\text{Eckert IV}$, where $t$ is the slider value. The blended maps are neither conformal nor equal-area, so the app only reports areas for the two ends of the slider. As a simple version of Tissot's indicatrix, the map can show 30 red circles, each with a radius of 3\dg{} on the globe. Drawn through the current projection, they stay round but grow towards the poles in Mercator, and keep their area but get squashed in Eckert IV (\cref{fig:p1-blend}).

\label{fig:p1-blend}

\paragraph{Area calculation.} The country boundaries are stored in PostGIS. The areas are computed once, in Python, and stored in a separate table. For each country, the script computes the area on a sphere ($R = 6\,371\,007$\,m) with pyproj, projects the boundary to Mercator and Eckert IV, and computes the planar area of each with Shapely. The area ratio is the projected area divided by the spherical area. The boundary lines are treated as great-circle arcs. Before projecting, they are split into segments of at most 5\,km, so that the projected outline follows the arcs. As a check, the ratio could change by less than $10^{-5}$ when going from 10\,km to 5\,km segments. Antarctica reaches the pole, where Mercator is undefined, so it is clipped at 85.05\dg{} and left out of the statistics. The script also computes $\sec^2\varphi$ at each country's centroid latitude, to compare the theory with the measured ratio.

\paragraph{Server side.} A small FastAPI service reads the countries and the areas from PostGIS and serves them to the map, and Docker Compose runs the database, the API and the web server together. The published website cannot run a server, so it uses a static copy of the same data (\cref{sec:website}).

\subsection{Results}

\Cref{tab:p1-top} shows the five countries that Mercator inflates the most. Over all countries except Antarctica, the median ratio is 1.21. In Eckert IV, every ratio is within $2.5\cdot10^{-6}$ of 1, which confirms that the calculation behaves as expected for an equal-area projection.

The chart in \cref{fig:p1-chart} plots each country's ratio against its centroid latitude, together with the $\sec^2\varphi$ curve. For 156 of 176 countries, the ratio is within 1\,\% of $\sec^2\varphi$ at the centroid. No country lies below the curve. The countries far above it span a large range of latitudes: Norway's ratio is 1.60 times its prediction (Svalbard reaches 80.7\dg{}N), Greenland's 1.57 and Canada's 1.50. The reason is that a country's ratio is an average of $\sec^2\varphi$ over its whole area. Since $\sec^2\varphi$ grows faster and faster towards the poles, the northern parts pull this average above the value at the centroid.

\subsection{Limitations}

The areas describe the simplified 1:110m boundaries, not official national areas. The ratios use a sphere; the WGS~84 ellipsoidal area of each country, which PostGIS also stores, differs from the spherical area by between $-0.45$\,\% and $+0.81$\,\%.
